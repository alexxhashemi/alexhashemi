Research

Speech recognition is increasingly how institutions hear people.

It captions classrooms, screens job interviews, routes calls to public services, and powers assistive devices. These systems are built and evaluated around a narrow idea of "standard" speech, and the people furthest from that standard, including people with speech disabilities, absorb the errors. My work treats those errors as evidence rather than noise.

Current project

My MA research is the first phase of a three-phase project:

  • Phase 1 · in progressMeasureA word-error-rate audit of automatic speech recognition bias across atypical speech corpora, establishing a reproducible baseline.
  • Phase 2TraceModelling how recognition errors travel through institutional decision pipelines.
  • Phase 3Evaluate withParticipatory evaluation with people with speech disabilities, so the standard reflects their communication rather than the system's.

Where errors become decisions

On the map, a sentence leaves home as "hi, i'm alex" and arrives as "hi, i'm a lax". On its own, that is a typo. Inside a school, an employer, or a public agency, it can become a missed accommodation, a failed screening, or a person marked as unclear. Through the When People Talk, Listen Completely project, I also work on communication access and employment barriers for people with speech impairment, where these consequences are concrete.
